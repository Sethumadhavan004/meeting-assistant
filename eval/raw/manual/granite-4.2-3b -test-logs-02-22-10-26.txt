Quantum entanglement is a strange but well-established phenomenon in quantum physics where two or more particles become linked in such a way that
their states are inseparable—no matter how far apart they are placed. Once entangled, measuring the state of one particle instantly determines the
state of the other(s), even if they are on opposite sides of the universe.

Here’s a simple way to understand it (without getting too technical):

- Imagine you have two magical dice that are entangled. If you roll one die and see it show a "3," you immediately know the other die will also show a
"3" (or whatever the paired outcome is), even if you’re on the Moon while holding the first die.
- The entanglement doesn’t work like regular objects (e.g., two regular dice that you could predict separately). Instead, their properties (like spin,
polarization, or quantum "orientation") are linked in an inseparable way, described by the rules of quantum mechanics.
- Crucially, **measurement of one particle doesn’t "send information" faster than light**—it only reveals a correlated result that was already linked.
This respects Einstein’s principle that relativity forbids faster-than-light signaling, even though the outcomes appear instantly correlated.

Key points to remember:
- Entanglement arises from quantum rules, not classical physics.
- It’s verified by experiments (e.g., Bell test experiments) and is a core part of quantum information science.
- It’s not about sending messages; it’s about the deep, non-local connection between particles described by quantum theory.
- Real-world examples include quantum cryptography (for secure communication), quantum computing (where entangled states enable powerful processing),
and emerging technologies like quantum networks.
